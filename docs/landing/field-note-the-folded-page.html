The folded page
does not need permission.

Why local-first software gives the user the same dignity as paper — and why "the cloud" is a bad answer when your reading habit is the subject.

2026.05.16 · MIZ ANANDI 10 min read · 2,140 words 5 footnotes · 3 glossary references tags · local-first · doctrine · paper

The folded page does not need permission to be written on. You open the notebook, your hand finds the pen, the ink lands on paper — and the entire question of whose notebook, whose server, whose uptime, simply does not arise. The page is yours because it is in front of you. Local-first software is the work of restoring that elementary dignity to digital writing.

For about fifteen years we let ourselves be talked into something strange: that our notes — drafts of letters, half-formed thoughts, reading marginalia — should live on someone else's computer, gated by an account, dependent on a network. The argument was always convenience. The reality, as everyone who has watched a service shut down can attest, was that we rented something we used to own.

What gets given up

When a notebook moves to the cloud, three quiet things are surrendered. The first is durability1 — your access to the words is gated by someone else's continued willingness to keep their server running. The second is privacy2 — your half-thoughts traverse the open internet, are stored on disks you do not control, and are read by systems you cannot audit. The third, less obvious, is latency of the soul3 — the small but real psychic delay between "I want to write" and "the input is ready."

"Software should outlive the company that made it." — from a captured tweet · field-note inbox · author unknown

The paper notebook concedes none of these. Its durability is on the order of decades; its privacy is whatever the lock on your drawer affords; its latency is the time it takes to open the cover. Sveska4 — the Bosnian word for notebook — is built on the wager that we can restore all three to the browser.

What "local-first" actually means

The phrase comes from a 2019 essay by Ink & Switch. The core claim is small: the source of truth lives on your device. Networks are optional. Sync, when it exists, is opt-in and end-to-end encrypted. The product still functions when the cloud does not.

In Sveska this looks like:

  • Your notes live in IndexedDB, in this browser, tied to this domain. No account required.
  • The AI proxy — the only network call the app makes — is optional. Disable it and the app loses one capability; it does not lose your notes.
  • Every snapshot, every tag, every preference is stored locally. Sveska can be opened tomorrow with the network unplugged and behave identically.
Local-first does not mean
offline-tolerant. It means
the network is the optional thing.

The folded page metaphor

The mark on the corner of Sveska's icon — the amber triangle, the dog-ear — is not decoration. It is the operative gesture of the system. To fold a page is to commit a thought; to unfold it is to return. The application's verbs are folding (saving a snapshot, marking a checklist item) and unfolding (restoring, browsing history, opening a snapshot from a month ago).

This is why snapshots5 are named, not numbered. The redo stack is loss-less; what the writer chooses to keep is not. A snapshot is not a save — it is a fold. You make one when you have decided that something is worth returning to.

What we trade away

Honesty requires admitting what local-first gives up. There are real things the cloud is good at — sharing, collaborative editing, mobile-desktop sync without thinking — that local-first either refuses outright or solves with more friction.

Sveska's answer is to be selective. We keep sharing (through URL-encoded payloads in the location hash, plaintext, opt-in, with a warning). We do AI (through a thin edge proxy whose only job is to be the place the key lives — never in your browser). We may eventually do sync (post-M6, only if E2E-encrypted, never speculatively). What we do not do is force any of these to be the price of opening the app.

One amber, no rainbow

A small note on aesthetics. Sveska's design system is built on a single accent color — amber, in three steps — and one signal color — cyan, only for live functional states. This is not minimalism. It is the same principle as the local-first one: what is not added is the biggest feature. A notebook with one accent is a notebook you can read for hours without the page yelling at you. The same restraint we ask of the network, we ask of the screen.

An empty notebook, in Bosnian, is prazna sveska. The phrase doubles as the empty-state copy and as a small private benediction we send to ourselves each time we open the app:

Prazna sveska.
Najbolji početak.

An empty notebook. The best beginning.
